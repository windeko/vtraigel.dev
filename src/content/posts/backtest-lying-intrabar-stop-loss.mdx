[Last time](/writing/tuning-a-trading-bot-1120-configs) I described the apparatus I use to pick this bot's parameters: 1,120 configs per coin, four data windows with four different jobs, pass criteria frozen in a design doc before any of it runs, and a relaxation ladder for when nothing passes. Trailing stops, post-stop cooldowns and multi-bar sell confirmation all died in that gate with zero winners across every coin.

I was proud of that gate. And every single decision it ever made ran against a backtest engine that modelled stop-losses wrong.

The mismatch is one line long. It was worth 28% of six years of simulated profit — and, worse, it had been quietly steering the parameter search toward configurations that only worked in the simulation.

## The mismatch

Production checks stop-loss like this: every 30 seconds, the cron wakes up, pulls the live ticker price, and asks "are we below the stop?" — unconditionally, before any signal logic runs. If the price is under the line at any point, the position is out.

The backtester checked stop-loss against the **close of the candle**.

On a 1h timeframe, those are very different questions. Consider a candle that opens at 100, wicks down to 94, and closes back at 99, with a stop sitting at 95. Production stopped out somewhere in the middle of that hour and booked the loss. The backtest looked at the close, saw 99, and reported a position that was never touched. A stop-out in reality; a phantom survival in the simulation.

I didn't build the fix first. I counted first — a cheap proxy over the historical data: bars where the low breaches the stop threshold but the close doesn't.

| Coin | Stop-loss | Phantom-survive bars per year (2025–26) |
|------|----------:|----------------------------------------:|
| DASH | 3% | 212 |
| SOL | 2% | 183 |
| DOT | 3% | 63 |
| FET | 4% | 63 |
| LTC | 3% | 61 |
| UNI | 4% | 47 |
| BTC / TRX | 4% | 2–5 |

Two hundred phantom survivals a year on a single coin. The bug wasn't uniform either — it was concentrated exactly on the tight-stop configs, which mattered more than I realised at the time.

## The fix, and the part everyone gets wrong

Detecting the stop on the candle's low is the obvious half. The fill price is the half worth thinking about:

```ts
if (params.intrabarStopLoss) {
  const low = candlesByCoin[coin][t][3];
  if (isStopLossHit(low, { buyPrice: pos.buyPrice }, strategy.config)) {
    const open = candlesByCoin[coin][t][1];
    const fill = Math.min(open, stopLossPrice(pos.buyPrice, strategy.config));
    portfolio.sell(coin, fill, t, 'STOP_LOSS');
    continue;
  }
}
```

`Math.min(open, stopLossPrice)` is the line that models reality. If the bar gapped open *below* the stop, my 30-second poll would have fired near that open — not politely at the stop level. Filling at the stop price in that case would have been a second, smaller lie layered on top of the first one. Four engine tests pin the behaviour: wick exit at the stop price, gap-down fill at the open, full-bar-below-threshold, and the legacy close-only path still intact behind a default-off flag.

I deliberately did **not** give take-profit the same treatment. In production, a take-profit exit also requires a live sell signal — five gates deep, rarely simultaneous with an intrabar touch. Modelling TP intrabar would need previous-bar signal semantics, a methodology fork that would break comparability with every result I'd already recorded. Leaving TP on close-only biases the backtest *pessimistic*, and when I can't model something exactly, I want the error pointing at the safe side.

## The damage

Same configs, same data, same six years. Only the stop-loss model changed.

| | Close-only stop | Intrabar stop | Δ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 6y PnL | +50.08% | +36.24% | **−28%** |
| Trades | 2,494 | 2,814 | +13% |
| 2024 | +9,233 | +7,730 | −16% |
| 2025 | +11,145 | +7,457 | **−33%** |
| 2022 (bear) | −2,796 | −5,131 | 84% worse |

Per coin, the spread was brutal:

| Coin | Stop | 6y PnL before | 6y PnL after | Sharpe |
|------|-----:|--------------:|-------------:|-------:|
| SOL | 2% | +7,923 | +1,413 | 1.30 → 0.36 |
| FET | 4% | +2,535 | **−926** | 0.44 → −0.12 |
| ONDO | 5% | +1,235 | +398 | 0.99 → 0.38 |
| LTC | 3% | +3,467 | +1,442 | 0.49 → 0.25 |
| BTC | 4% | +3,069 | +2,313 | 0.65 → 0.51 |
| DASH | 3% | +5,422 | +5,311 | 0.75 (flat) |
| DOGE | 5% | +11,166 | +12,116 | 0.59 → 0.63 |

SOL lost 82% of its simulated profit. FET went from a profitable slot to a losing one. SOL's 2022 slid from −0.44% to −14.21% — it would have failed the bear-year gate it had never actually been subjected to.

And DOGE got *better*, which is the detail that convinced me the new model was right rather than just gloomier. When a bar closes below the stop, the close-only model filled at that worse close; the intrabar model fills at the stop level. For wide-stop coins, that fill-price correction outweighs the extra stop-outs. A model that only ever made things worse would have been suspicious. This one moved results in both directions, for reasons I could trace.

## The finding that actually mattered

My first instinct was to treat this as a haircut: same portfolio, lower expectations, move on.

That instinct was wrong, and it took me an embarrassing beat to see why. **Every parameter in production had been selected by sweeps running on the broken model.** Tight stops didn't just look better than they were — they won their sweeps *because* phantom wick-survival was free in the simulation. The stop-loss values in my config weren't merely optimistic. They were selected *for* the bug.

So I re-ran the whole search: 1,120 configs per coin, twelve coins, sequentially, under the honest model, benchmarked against the current production config on the same honest model.

Five coins promoted new configs, and they all moved in the same direction: **wider stop, quicker take-profit, deeper retrace on entry.** SOL's stop went 2% → 4%, LTC's 3% → 5%. Take-profits came down: LINK 10% → 5%, LTC 7% → 3%, SOL 5% → 3%. ONDO changed strategy family outright.

The tell was in the two coins that barely moved. TRX and DASH lost 13% and 2% to the correction — nothing, compared to SOL's 82%. Both were already sitting on the wide-stop/quick-TP shape. They were the coins whose parameters had never depended on the bug, so honest simulation had nothing to take away from them.

After promotions, six-year PnL recovered from +36.24% to +39.73%, and the bear-year loss more than halved, from −5,131 to −2,222. Still well below the +50.08% I thought I had. That gap was never profit; it was measurement error I'd been reading as profit.

## Three coins had no answer at all

Three coins produced **zero** winners out of 1,120 configs each — nothing in the family passed the gate under honest simulation.

BTC's candidates were all strong on train and negative on forward: a regime break, not a tuning problem. DOT sat at forward −3.14%. UNI was the uncomfortable one: forward −9.90%, and a 2022 loss of −13.66% that fails my own −10% bear-year gate. Worse, UNI had been *promoted to the largest position tier* three weeks earlier on the strength of a "+2.95% 2026" number — a close-only number. Under honest simulation that year is negative.

So the position sizes moved instead of the parameters. UNI cut from the top tier to the minimum, BTC down to minimum as a portfolio anchor, DOGE down a tier — its profit is real but concentrated in a single year, which the top tier didn't deserve. DOT stayed at minimum, where it already was.

Cutting size on a coin because your simulator got more honest is a strange feeling. It's also the only correct response.

## What I took from it

**Every simulator is optimistic in a direction. Find yours and name it.** Mine was: intra-candle price movement doesn't exist. That's a modelling choice I never consciously made — it was implicit in reading `candle.close`, which is the kind of decision that never gets reviewed because it never looks like a decision.

**When you fix a fidelity bug, re-run the search, not just the numbers.** Re-reading old results under a new model tells you how wrong you were. It doesn't fix anything. The parameters themselves were artifacts of the broken model, and no amount of re-reading turns a selected-for-the-bug config into a good one.

**Prefer the model that errs conservative when you can't model exactly.** Intrabar mode slightly *overestimates* stop frequency — a 30-second poll can miss a sub-30-second wick that the candle low records. Truth sits between the two models, much closer to intrabar. I'd rather be a little too pessimistic about my own bot than a little too optimistic.

**Fidelity beats cleverness.** I've tested a lot of strategy ideas against this bot. Trailing stops: no winners. Post-stop cooldown: no winners. Multi-bar sell confirmation: no winners, and one textbook overfit caught by the out-of-sample criterion. One boring correctness fix to the simulation moved more than all of them combined — in both directions, on every coin, permanently.

The most valuable work on a trading bot isn't the strategy. It's making sure the numbers you're reading are about the thing you actually run.
